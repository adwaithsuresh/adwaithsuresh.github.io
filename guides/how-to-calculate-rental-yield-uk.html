How to calculate rental yield UK: gross yield, net yield and a worked example

Updated 2026-10-14

Gross yield

Gross rental yield is the annual rent divided by the price of the property, multiplied by 100 to give a percentage.

A flat bought for GBP 200,000 and let at GBP 1,000 a month brings in GBP 12,000 a year, so its gross yield is 12,000 divided by 200,000, which is 6 percent.

Gross yield is quick to work out from a listing, which makes it useful for comparing properties, but it ignores every cost of buying and running the let.

Net yield and which costs to include

Net yield takes the yearly running costs off the rent first, then divides by the total cost of buying, including stamp duty, solicitor, survey and any refurbishment.

Running costs usually include letting or management fees, landlord insurance, repairs and maintenance, ground rent and service charge on a leasehold flat, safety certificates, any licence fee your council charges, and rent lost to empty weeks between tenants.

There is no single official definition, and people often leave mortgage interest out of net yield and look at it separately as cash flow, so when you compare figures from different sources, check what each one includes.

Stamp duty on a buy to let

In England and Northern Ireland, buying an additional home such as a buy to let usually means paying the higher rates of stamp duty, which add 5 percentage points to each band from 31 October 2024.

From 1 April 2025 the standard residential bands are 0 percent up to GBP 125,000, 2 percent to GBP 250,000, 5 percent to GBP 925,000, 10 percent to GBP 1.5 million and 12 percent above that, so with the surcharge a GBP 200,000 purchase costs GBP 6,250 on the first GBP 125,000 plus GBP 5,250 on the next GBP 75,000, which is GBP 11,500.

Scotland charges Land and Buildings Transaction Tax with its own Additional Dwelling Supplement, and Wales charges Land Transaction Tax with its own higher rates, so use the Revenue Scotland or Welsh Revenue Authority calculator there.

Rates change, so always check the current figure on GOV.UK before an offer.

A worked example

Take the same GBP 200,000 flat at GBP 1,000 a month, with GBP 11,500 stamp duty and an assumed GBP 2,500 for solicitor and survey, which makes GBP 214,000 in total.

Assume yearly running costs of GBP 1,440 for a 12 percent letting fee, GBP 1,200 for repairs, GBP 400 for insurance and safety checks, and about GBP 460 for two empty weeks, which comes to GBP 3,500.

The rent left is GBP 8,500, and 8,500 divided by 214,000 gives a net yield of about 4 percent, against 6 percent gross.

These costs are made up for the example, so swap in real quotes for the property you are looking at.

Mortgage, cash flow and the lender stress test

Yield measures the property, while cash flow measures your own position after the mortgage payment, so a deal with a decent yield can still lose money each month if the mortgage is large or the rate rises.

Buy to let lenders usually size the loan from the rent using an interest cover ratio, often 125 percent or 145 percent of the interest worked out at a stress rate set by the lender, so the rent can limit how much you borrow.

It is worth rerunning your figures with the rate 1, 2 or 3 points higher to see how much room the deal has.

Tax and running costs to plan for

Individual landlords cannot deduct residential mortgage interest from rental income as an expense, and instead get a tax credit worth 20 percent of the finance costs, so yield before tax can look much better than what a higher rate taxpayer keeps.

Rental profit is reported through Self Assessment for the tax year from 6 April to 5 April, with the online return due by 31 January.

In England you need a gas safety check every 12 months, an electrical safety report (EICR) at least every 5 years, and a valid EPC, and tenancy deposits must be protected in a government approved scheme within 30 days.
