How many days annual leave am I entitled to in the UK? A plain guide to working it out

Updated 2026-10-07

The legal minimum is 5.6 weeks a year

Almost all UK workers are entitled to at least 5.6 weeks of paid holiday a year, known as statutory annual leave.

For someone working 5 days a week that is 28 days, because 5 multiplied by 5.6 is 28.

The statutory figure is capped at 28 days, so someone working 6 days a week still gets 28 days by law, although their contract can give more.

Your contract or staff handbook may offer more than the minimum, so check it first, as that is the figure you should be tracking.

Bank holidays can count towards the 28 days

There is no legal right to have bank holidays off, and your employer can include them in your 28 days.

England and Wales have 8 bank holidays a year, Scotland 9 and Northern Ireland 10, so a 28 days including bank holidays contract in England leaves you 20 days to choose yourself.

In 2027 the England and Wales bank holidays are 1 January, 26 March (Good Friday), 29 March (Easter Monday), 3 May, 31 May, 30 August, and 27 and 28 December, because Christmas Day and Boxing Day fall on a weekend.

Read the wording carefully, as 25 days plus bank holidays and 25 days including bank holidays are very different deals.

Part time workers: multiply your days by 5.6

If you work a fixed number of days each week, multiply those days by 5.6 to get your statutory entitlement.

Someone working 3 days a week gets 16.8 days, and someone working 4 days gets 22.4 days, which employers may round up but cannot round down.

Part timers should not be treated less favourably than full timers, so if your employer gives full timers extra days on top of the minimum, you should normally get a pro rata share of those too.

If you start or leave partway through the leave year, your entitlement for that year is worked out in proportion to the part of the year you were employed.

Irregular hours and part year workers

For leave years starting on or after 1 April 2024, irregular hours and part year workers build up holiday at 12.07% of the hours they actually work in each pay period.

For example, working 100 hours in a month builds up about 12 hours of holiday.

For these workers, employers can choose to pay holiday as an extra 12.07% on each payslip, called rolled up holiday pay, instead of paying it when the time off is taken.

If your hours vary, keep your own record of hours worked so you can check the holiday you have built up.

When you can take it and how much notice to give

Your leave year is set by your contract, and if the contract does not say, it usually runs from the date you started the job.

Unless your contract sets other rules, you must give notice of at least twice the length of the holiday, so 2 weeks' notice for 1 week off.

Your employer can refuse a request by giving notice at least as long as the leave asked for, and can also tell you when to take leave, for example over a Christmas shutdown, with notice of twice the length.

Employers cannot pay you instead of taking statutory leave, except for holiday you have not taken when you leave the job.

Carrying leave over into the next year

Statutory leave is meant to be taken in the leave year it belongs to, and many employers have a use it or lose it rule.

Your contract may let you carry over some days, and the law allows up to 1.6 weeks, 8 days for a 5 day week, to be carried over by agreement.

If you could not take your leave because you were off sick or on maternity, paternity, adoption or shared parental leave, you can usually carry it over into the next leave year.

Check the carry over rule a few months before your leave year ends, so you are not left with days you cannot use.

Working out how many days you have left

Start with your total entitlement for the year, add any days carried over, then take off every day already taken and every day booked but not yet taken.

Count only working days in each booking, so a Monday to Friday week off is 5 days, and a week that includes a bank holiday you already get off is 4.

Half days add up quickly, so record them as 0.5 rather than rounding.
