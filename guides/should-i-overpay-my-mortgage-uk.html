Should I overpay my mortgage UK: allowances, charges and what it saves

Updated 2026-09-25

What an overpayment does

An overpayment is any money you pay towards your mortgage on top of the normal monthly payment, either as a regular extra amount or as a one-off lump sum.

It goes straight off the balance you owe, so every later month charges interest on a smaller amount.

That is why small regular overpayments made early in a mortgage save more than the same total paid in near the end.

The overpayment allowance and early repayment charges

Most fixed rate and tracker deals let you overpay up to a set limit each year without a penalty, and 10 percent of the balance a year is a common limit.

Pay more than the allowance during the deal and the lender can apply an early repayment charge (ERC), usually a percentage of the amount repaid that often steps down each year of the deal.

Lenders differ on whether the allowance is measured from the balance at the start of the deal or of each year, and on when the year resets, so check your mortgage offer or ask the lender before a large payment.

On a standard variable rate, once a fixed deal has ended, there is usually no early repayment charge at all.

Shorter term or lower payments

After an overpayment, lenders either keep your monthly payment the same and shorten the term, or keep the term and lower the monthly payment.

Keeping the payment the same saves the most interest, because the whole of the normal payment keeps working on a smaller balance.

Lenders handle this differently, and some let you choose, so it is worth asking which one they will apply.

A worked example

Take a GBP 200,000 repayment mortgage at 4.5 percent over 25 years, which costs about GBP 1,112 a month.

Adding GBP 100 a month from the start, with the rate staying at 4.5 percent and the payment kept the same, clears it roughly three and a half years early.

Over the life of the mortgage that is about GBP 21,000 less interest, from GBP 30,000 of overpayments.

Real results will differ because rates change when deals end and lenders work out interest in their own way, so treat any figure like this as an estimate.

Overpaying compared with saving

A simple comparison is your mortgage rate against the rate you could earn on savings after tax, since each pound overpaid saves interest at the mortgage rate.

Savings interest is tax free up to your Personal Savings Allowance, which is GBP 1,000 a year for basic rate taxpayers, GBP 500 for higher rate and nothing for additional rate, and interest inside a Cash ISA is tax free within the GBP 20,000 yearly ISA allowance.

Money paid into a mortgage is usually hard to get back unless your deal lets you borrow back overpayments, so many people keep an emergency fund in easy access savings first.

Credit cards and loans often charge more interest than a mortgage, which is why people commonly clear those before overpaying.

Overpaying before a remortgage

Mortgage rates are priced in loan to value (LTV) bands, the balance as a percentage of the property value.

Overpaying in the run-up to the end of a fixed deal can take you below a band edge such as 75 percent or 60 percent, which may open up lower rates when you remortgage.

Work out your balance at the end of the fix and the property value you expect, and see how far you are from the next band down.

Tracking it yourself

Keep a note of each overpayment and its date, and add up how much you have paid in each year of your deal so you stay inside the allowance.
