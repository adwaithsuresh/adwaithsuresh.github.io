Do I have to pay tax on selling online in the UK? The GBP 1,000 rule explained

Updated 2026-10-12

Selling your own unwanted things

If you are clearing out your wardrobe or loft and selling things you bought for your own use, that is usually not trading, and there is normally no income tax to pay on it.

Most of these items sell for less than you paid, so there is no profit to tax in the first place.

Personal possessions sold for GBP 6,000 or less are also generally free of Capital Gains Tax, so a few higher value items are not usually a problem either.

It helps to keep a simple note of what you sold and that it was your own, in case you ever need to show the difference.

When selling becomes trading

HMRC looks at what it calls the badges of trade to decide whether you are running a business.

The main signs are buying things with the plan of selling them at a profit, selling regularly or in volume, and repairing, altering or making items before you sell them.

Buying stock from car boot sales, charity shops or clearance rails to sell on is the classic example of trading, even if it is only a side hustle.

No single sign decides it, so if you are unsure where you sit, the GOV.UK guidance on selling online is the place to check.

The GBP 1,000 trading allowance

If you are trading, the first GBP 1,000 of trading income in a tax year is covered by the trading allowance, and if your total is under that you do not need to tell HMRC about it.

The figure is your gross takings before any costs, not your profit, so GBP 1,200 of sales counts as over the allowance even if the stock cost you GBP 900.

If you go over, you can either deduct the GBP 1,000 allowance or deduct your actual costs, such as stock, postage, packaging and selling fees, but not both.

The allowance is per person and covers all your trading income together, so sales from a second side hustle are added to it.

The platform reporting rules

Since 1 January 2024, online marketplaces have had to collect details of their sellers and send HMRC a yearly report of what each seller earned.

A platform does not have to report you if you sold fewer than 30 items and received about EUR 2,000 (roughly GBP 1,700) or less on that platform in the calendar year.

These rules did not create a new tax, and being reported does not mean you owe anything, because someone clearing out their own belongings can pass the threshold.

Note that the reporting threshold runs on the calendar year, January to December, while the trading allowance runs on the tax year from 6 April to 5 April.

If you need to register

If your trading income goes over GBP 1,000 in a tax year, you need to register for Self Assessment by 5 October after that tax year ends.

For the 2026/27 tax year, which runs from 6 April 2026 to 5 April 2027, that means registering by 5 October 2027.

The online return and any tax owed are then due by 31 January, so 31 January 2028 for 2026/27.

If you have any doubt about your own position, check GOV.UK or ask an accountant, as this guide is general information rather than tax advice.

Keep records as you go

Whichever side of the line you are on, the easiest protection is a record of what you bought, what it cost, what it sold for, and the fees and postage on each sale.

Mark items that were your own unwanted things separately, so they are not mixed in with stock you bought to sell.

Keep a running count per platform for the calendar year and a total of trading takings for the tax year, so you can see where you stand against both figures.
