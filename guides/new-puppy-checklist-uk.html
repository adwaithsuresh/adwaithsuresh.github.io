New puppy checklist UK: what to sort before and after your puppy comes home

Updated 2026-10-13

Before you choose a puppy

In England, Lucy's Law means a puppy under six months old must be bought directly from the breeder who bred it or adopted from a rescue centre, not from a pet shop or other third-party dealer.

Ask to see the puppy with its mother where it was born, and walk away from anyone who offers to meet you in a car park or deliver the puppy to your door.

In England, anyone who breeds three or more litters in a 12 month period, or who breeds and sells as a business, needs a licence from their local council, so it is fair to ask a breeder for their licence number.

Rescue centres usually do a home check and will tell you what the dog has already had done, which saves some of the steps below.

Microchip and ID tag: the legal basics

Across the UK, dogs must be microchipped, and in England, Scotland and Wales the breeder must have the puppy chipped by the time it is eight weeks old, before it goes to a new home.

Once the puppy is yours, the keeper details on the microchip database must be updated to your name and address, and kept up to date if you move.

Separately, the Control of Dogs Order 1992 requires a dog in a public place to wear a collar with the owner's name and address on it or on an attached tag, and a phone number is a sensible extra.

Write down the chip number and which database it is registered with on the day you collect your puppy, because you will need both if the dog is ever lost.

Vet, health records and insurance

Register with a local vet before collection day, and book a first check in the first week so any problems are found while you can still talk to the breeder.

Ask the breeder or rescue for the puppy's records of vaccinations, worming and flea treatment so far, and let your vet set the schedule from there rather than guessing.

Your vet will also tell you when it is safe for the puppy to walk in public places, so plan carrying trips and garden time until then.

Pet insurance is not a legal requirement, but there is no free public cover for animals and treatment can run into thousands of pounds, so compare lifetime, time-limited and accident-only policies before the puppy arrives, and check whether third party liability is included.

What to buy before collection day

The basics are a crate or pen, a bed, food and water bowls, the same food the puppy is already eating, a collar and tag, a lead, poo bags, puppy pads or an outdoor toilet spot, chew toys and an enzyme cleaner for accidents.

A stair gate and a sweep of the house for cables, shoes, cleaning products and poisonous plants will prevent most early damage and vet trips.

Change food gradually if you change it at all, since a sudden switch on top of a house move is a common cause of upset stomachs.

Keep receipts in one place for the first month, because the setup costs are usually higher than people expect and it helps to see the real total.

The first weeks at home

Puppies do best on a simple routine of meals, toilet trips, play and naps at roughly the same times each day, with a toilet trip after every meal, nap and play session.

Socialisation in the early months matters more than any single command, so gently introduce everyday UK sights and sounds such as buses, the school run, bin lorries, visitors at the door and other calm dogs once your vet says it is safe.

Start with short training sessions on their name, sit, recall and settling in a crate, and note what works so everyone in the house uses the same words.

Under the Dangerous Dogs Act 1991 it is an offence to let any dog be dangerously out of control, in public or in a private place, which is a good reason to start recall and lead work early.

Fireworks season and the countryside

A puppy arriving in October or November will meet Bonfire Night and New Year fireworks very early, so prepare a quiet den, close curtains, play background sound, and walk earlier in the day.

If your puppy is very frightened by noises, speak to your vet rather than trying remedies on your own.

In the countryside, keep your dog on a lead around sheep, cattle and horses, because letting a dog worry livestock on agricultural land is an offence and farmers may be entitled to protect their animals.
