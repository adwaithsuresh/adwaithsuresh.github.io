What to put in a wedding budget UK: every cost to plan for, including the hidden ones

Updated 2026-10-08

Start with the total and the guest number

Before listing suppliers, agree two numbers together: the most you are willing to spend in total, and roughly how many day and evening guests you want.

Guest numbers drive most of the bill, because catering, drinks, chairs, stationery and favours are all priced per head, and they also decide which venues are big enough.

Write down where the money is coming from as well, such as savings, monthly saving between now and the date, and any family contributions, so the total is something you can actually reach.

The main categories to include

Ceremony and legal costs, venue hire, food and drink for the day and the evening, and the wedding outfits, including alterations, shoes, accessories and hair and make-up.

Photography and video, flowers and decoration, music or entertainment, the cake, stationery (save the dates, invitations, order of service, place cards and postage) and transport.

Rings, gifts for the wedding party, accommodation for the night itself, and the honeymoon if you want it inside the same budget.

Hen and stag costs are usually paid by the guests, but if you are covering any of your own share or anything for the wedding party, give it its own line.

Legal costs and giving notice

In England and Wales, for a civil ceremony both partners normally give notice of marriage in person at a register office at least 29 days before the wedding, and each pays a notice fee.

If the ceremony is at an approved venue rather than the register office, there is also a fee for the registrars to attend, which is set by the local council and varies from area to area, so ask the council for the current figure.

Church of England and Church in Wales weddings usually use banns instead of giving notice, and Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own processes, so check GOV.UK or nidirect for the rules that apply to you.

Add a small amount for copies of the marriage certificate, which you may need for changing your name on bank accounts and a passport.

The venue extras people miss

Read the venue contract line by line: many venues have a minimum spend, a service charge added to food and drink, and fees for corkage or for cutting and serving a cake from an outside supplier.

Check whether quotes include VAT, which is charged at 20 percent by VAT registered suppliers, since a quote shown plus VAT is a fifth higher than it first looks.

Smaller suppliers below the VAT threshold do not charge it, which is one reason quotes for the same job can look so different.

Also ask about room hire for the evening, extra staff after a set time, and any charge for setting up and clearing your own decorations.

Plan payment dates, not just totals

Most suppliers take a deposit when you book and the balance a few weeks before the day, so a budget that only shows totals can still leave you short in the final two months.

For each supplier, note the deposit, the balance, the date it is due and whether the deposit is refundable, then add up what falls due in each month.

Paying for a single item costing over GBP 100 and up to GBP 30,000 partly or wholly on a credit card can give you extra protection under Section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act if a supplier fails to deliver, which is worth knowing for large deposits.

Keep a buffer and track it as you go

Set aside a contingency line rather than spreading every pound across suppliers, because late guests, postage, alterations and last-minute items add up in the final weeks.

Track each category as quoted, paid and left to pay, so you can see straight away when one area has gone over and decide where to take it back from.
