How to make a GCSE revision timetable that you will actually stick to

Updated 2026-10-03

Start with your exam dates, not a blank grid

Ask your school for your mock dates and, later in the year, your personal exam timetable, and write every paper on one page before you plan anything else.

Mocks are usually set by each school, often between November and January, while the summer GCSE exams run from May into June.

Count the weeks between today and your first paper, because that number decides how often each subject can come round.

List every subject and break it into topics

Write down each subject you are sitting, then split each one into the topics on your specification, which your teacher or the exam board website can give you.

Rate each topic red, amber or green: red for topics you cannot explain, amber for shaky, green for confident.

This list is the real plan, as the timetable only decides when you work through the reds and ambers.

Decide how much time you really have each week

Block out school, travel, clubs, a part time job, meals and sleep first, then look at what is left on a normal weekday and at weekends.

Short sessions of 30 to 45 minutes with a short break between them are easier to start and to keep up than long evenings.

Be honest about this step, since a timetable built on hours you do not have is the main reason plans get abandoned in the second week.

Fill the week so subjects come round often

Give each subject at least one slot a week, and give more slots to subjects with more papers or more red topics.

Mix subjects across the week rather than doing all of one subject in a single day, as coming back to a topic after a few days helps it stick.

Put each session down as a topic, such as "Biology: cell division", not just "Biology", so you know exactly what to open when you sit down.

Use active revision, not just re-reading

Test yourself with flashcards, blank page recall (write everything you remember, then check your notes) or a past paper question at the end of each session.

Past papers and mark schemes from the exam board websites show you how marks are given, and they are free.

After each past paper, note the mark and the one or two things to fix, then add those to next week's plan.

Leave a catch up slot and review every Sunday

Keep one slot a week empty so a missed evening can move there instead of knocking the whole plan over.

Spend ten minutes at the end of the week recolouring your topic list and moving next week's sessions towards whatever is still red.
