Gas mark to Celsius: oven temperature conversions for UK cooks

Updated 2026-10-10

Gas mark to Celsius and Fahrenheit, 1 to 9

Gas mark 1 is 140 C (275 F), gas mark 2 is 150 C (300 F) and gas mark 3 is 170 C (325 F), though some charts round gas mark 3 down to 160 C.

Gas mark 4 is 180 C (350 F), gas mark 5 is 190 C (375 F) and gas mark 6 is 200 C (400 F).

Gas mark 7 is 220 C (425 F), gas mark 8 is 230 C (450 F) and gas mark 9 is 240 C (475 F).

For very slow cooking, gas mark 1/4 is about 110 C (225 F) and gas mark 1/2 is about 120 C (250 F).

Fan ovens: knock off about 20 degrees

A fan oven moves hot air around the food, so it cooks faster than a conventional electric or gas oven at the same setting.

The usual rule is to set a fan oven about 20 C lower than the conventional temperature, so a recipe at 180 C becomes 160 C fan.

Many UK recipes now print both figures, written as 180 C or 160 C fan, and if only one is given it is usually the conventional one.

Ovens vary, so check the manual for your model and keep an eye on the first bake at a new temperature.

Converting Fahrenheit yourself

To turn Fahrenheit into Celsius, subtract 32, multiply by 5, then divide by 9.

For example, 350 F minus 32 is 318, times 5 is 1,590, and divided by 9 is about 177 C, which recipes round to 180 C.

To go the other way, multiply the Celsius figure by 9, divide by 5, then add 32.

American recipes almost always give Fahrenheit for a conventional oven, so take off the fan adjustment after converting.

Ounces, pints and spoons

One ounce is 28.35 grams, so most British cookbooks round it to 25 g or 30 g, and a pound (16 ounces) is 454 g.

A UK pint is 568 ml but a US pint is 473 ml, which matters in older family recipes and in anything written in America.

A tablespoon is 15 ml and a teaspoon is 5 ml in UK and US recipes alike, so spoon measures can be used as written.

Why cups are hard to convert

A US cup is a measure of volume, about 240 ml, not weight, so a cup of flour and a cup of sugar weigh quite different amounts.

How tightly the cup is packed changes the weight too, which is why baking from cups gives less reliable results than weighing.

If an American recipe gives grams alongside cups, use the grams, and if it does not, look for a conversion for that specific ingredient rather than a general one.

Writing recipes down so they work next time

When you copy out a family recipe, write the oven temperature three ways (Celsius, fan and gas mark) so it works whatever oven you or a relative have.

Note any changes you made, such as a shorter cooking time in your oven, next to the method while you still remember them.
