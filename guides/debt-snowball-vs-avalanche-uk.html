Debt snowball vs avalanche UK: which order to pay off your debts

Updated 2026-09-26

The two methods in one paragraph each

Both methods start the same way: pay the minimum on every debt, then put every spare pound you can find towards one target debt until it is cleared.

With the snowball, the target is the debt with the smallest balance. When it is gone, its payment rolls into the next smallest, so the amount you throw at each debt grows as you go.

With the avalanche, the target is the debt with the highest interest rate, whatever its balance. Everything else works the same way.

The avalanche always costs the same or less in interest. The snowball gives you a paid off debt sooner, which many people find keeps them going.

A simple example

Say you owe GBP 600 on a store card at 30%, GBP 2,500 on a credit card at 24%, and GBP 6,000 on a personal loan at 9%.

The snowball order is store card, credit card, loan, because that is smallest balance first. The avalanche order is store card, credit card, loan too, because that is also highest rate first.

When the two orders match, there is nothing to decide. The choice only matters when a small debt has a low rate, for example a GBP 400 interest free loan from a retailer alongside a large credit card at 25%.

In that case the avalanche says clear the credit card first, and the snowball says clear the GBP 400 first. The gap in interest is usually small when the small debt is small, so pick the one you will actually stick with.

Priority debts come before either method

UK debt advice separates priority debts from the rest. Priority debts are the ones where missing payments can cost you your home, your energy supply or your liberty, or lead to court action.

They include rent or mortgage arrears, council tax, gas and electricity, court fines, child maintenance, Income Tax or VAT owed to HMRC, and the TV licence.

Keep these up to date before you put anything extra towards credit cards, overdrafts or loans, even if a card has a higher rate.

If you are behind on priority debts, free debt advice from a charity or the government backed MoneyHelper service is the right first step, not a spreadsheet.

UK debts that usually stay out of the plan

Student loans for England and Wales are repaid through payroll as a percentage of what you earn above a threshold, and any balance left is written off after a set number of years. Paying them off early rarely makes sense, so most people leave them out.

A 0% balance transfer or 0% purchase card costs nothing while the offer lasts. Put it in the plan with its real rate from the date the offer ends, and aim to clear it before then.

Your mortgage is normally left out too. Overpaying a mortgage is a separate decision, and early repayment charges can apply above the allowance in your deal.

UK rules worth knowing while you pay down cards

UK credit card minimum payments are usually set to cover at least the interest and charges plus 1% of the balance, which is why paying only the minimum takes so long.

Under FCA rules, if you have paid more in interest, fees and charges than you have repaid of the balance over 18 months, your card provider must contact you about it. After 36 months it must offer you a way to clear the balance in a reasonable time.

Since April 2020, arranged overdrafts are charged as a single interest rate rather than daily or monthly fees, so you can compare an overdraft with a card on its rate.

How to set up your plan

List every non priority debt with its balance, interest rate and minimum payment, taken from your latest statements rather than from memory.

Work out one fixed monthly amount you can put towards debt in total, and keep that figure the same even as debts clear, so freed up minimums roll into the next target.

Check progress once a month, update the balances, and do not add new borrowing to a card you have already cleared.
