Sinking fund ideas UK: the yearly costs worth saving for each month

Updated 2026-09-23

What a sinking fund is

A sinking fund is money you put aside every month for a cost you already know is coming, so it does not land as a shock when the bill arrives.

Most UK budgets cope with the monthly direct debits but get knocked off course by the yearly and seasonal costs: the car, the boiler, Christmas, the school uniform in August.

The fix is to list those costs, spread each one over the months before it is due, and keep the money separate from your everyday spending.

Car and travel pots

MOT: once a car is three years old in Great Britain (four in Northern Ireland) it needs an MOT every year, and any repairs it flags often cost more than the test itself.

Car service, tyres and vehicle tax: paying vehicle tax by Direct Debit in instalments usually costs a little more than paying for the year in one go, so saving ahead can let you pay upfront.

Car insurance: paying monthly usually includes a credit charge, so many people save through the year and pay the annual premium at renewal.

Breakdown cover, a railcard, and parking permits are small yearly costs that are easy to forget.

Home pots

Council tax is normally collected in 10 monthly instalments from April, and you have the right to ask your council to spread it over 12 instead, which is worth knowing when you plan the year.

Home and contents insurance, the annual boiler service, and a TV licence (needed to watch live TV or use BBC iPlayer) all come round once a year.

If you own a leasehold flat, ground rent and service charges are often billed once or twice a year, and they can be large.

A general home repairs pot covers the things you cannot date, such as a broken washing machine or a leaking roof.

Family and seasonal pots

Christmas is the classic sinking fund: add up last year's gifts, food and travel, then divide by the months left until December.

Birthdays, school uniform and shoes before September, school trips, and summer holidays are all predictable costs with known months.

Pets deserve their own pot for vaccinations, insurance renewals and vet bills that insurance does not cover.

Eye tests, glasses and dental treatment are worth a small monthly amount too, since they tend to arrive together.

Pots people often forget

If you are self-employed or have income taxed through self assessment, the balancing payment is due on 31 January and any payment on account on 31 July, so a tax pot saves a painful January.

Annual subscriptions, professional memberships and software renewals often renew automatically on a card you have stopped watching.

Replacing a phone or laptop every few years is a sinking fund in disguise, and so is a pot for a future car.

How to work out the monthly amount

For each pot, take the expected cost and divide it by the number of months until it is due, not by 12, otherwise a pot that starts late will fall short.

For example, a GBP 480 insurance renewal due in 8 months needs GBP 60 a month, and once it is paid the amount drops back to the full-year figure of GBP 40 a month.

Many people keep the money in a separate savings account or in named pots within their bank app, so it is not spent by accident.
