How to plan a dissertation timeline: a week by week method for UK students

Updated 2026-10-14

Start from the hand in date and work backwards

Find the exact hand in date and time in your module handbook, then count the weeks between today and that date.

For most UK final year undergraduates the topic is chosen in the autumn term and the dissertation is handed in around April or May, so October is a good moment to set the plan.

Plan each stage backwards from the deadline, not forwards from today, because the late stages such as proofreading and formatting are the ones that get squeezed.

Leave at least one spare week near the end for illness, a late chapter or a printing or upload problem.

The stages to put on the timeline

A typical dissertation runs through topic and research question, proposal, ethics application if needed, literature review, methodology, data collection, analysis, findings, discussion, conclusion, abstract, then proofreading and submission.

Give each stage a planned start date, a planned end date and a done date, so you can see straight away when you are slipping.

Some stages overlap, for example you can keep reading for the literature review while waiting for ethics approval, so do not assume they must run one after another.

Write the abstract and the introduction last, once you know what the dissertation actually found.

Do not leave ethics approval until it blocks you

If your research involves people, such as interviews, surveys or observations, your university will usually require ethics approval before you collect any data.

Approval can take several weeks and may come back with changes, so submit the application as early as your supervisor allows.

Your department's own ethics process and forms are the only ones that count, so check them rather than relying on a general guide.

Split the word limit across chapters

Many UK undergraduate dissertations are set at around 10,000 words, but limits vary a lot by course, so use the figure in your handbook.

Some universities allow a margin either side of the limit, often 10 percent, while others treat the limit as a hard cap, so check before relying on it.

A common starting split is about 10 percent for the introduction, 30 percent for the literature review, 15 percent for methodology, 20 percent each for findings and discussion, and 5 percent for the conclusion.

Adjust that split to your subject and your supervisor's advice, then track words written against each chapter target every week.

Keep a literature matrix from the first source

Record every source as you read it, with author, year, title, where it was published, page numbers, the main findings, limitations and how relevant it is to your question.

Tag each source with one or more themes, because a good literature review is organised by theme or argument rather than as a list of summaries.

Write down quotes with the page number at the time, since finding a page number again in April is slow and frustrating.

Use the referencing style your course asks for, which in many UK departments is a version of Harvard, and check every reference against your university's own guide.

Use supervisor meetings to keep the plan honest

Before each meeting, send or bring a short note of what you have done since last time and what you want to ask.

After each meeting, write down the agreed actions with a date against each one, and tick them off before the next meeting.

If the timeline has slipped, say so early, because supervisors can usually help you cut scope in January far more easily than in April.

Plan the last fortnight in detail

Set aside the final one to two weeks for proofreading, checking that spelling is consistent, that every in text citation appears in the reference list, and that figures and tables are numbered in order.

Check the word count against the rules in your handbook, including whether the reference list, appendices and tables count towards it.

Confirm the submission format, whether that is an online upload, a printed and bound copy, or both, and do a test upload if the system allows it.
