What to do when someone dies in the UK: a step by step checklist

Updated 2026-10-13

The first few days

If the death was expected and happened at home, call the GP or the hospice team, and if it was unexpected, call 999.

A doctor will complete a medical certificate of cause of death, which you need before you can register the death, and in some cases the death is referred to a coroner first, which can delay things.

You can contact a funeral director straight away, but you do not need to book the funeral date until you know when the death will be registered.

Start a single list of who you have told, with the date and any reference number, as you will be asked the same questions many times.

Register the death

In England, Wales and Northern Ireland you should register a death within 5 days, and in Scotland within 8 days, at the register office for the area where the person died unless the registrar tells you otherwise.

Take the medical certificate and, if you have them, the person's birth certificate, NHS number, National Insurance number and any marriage or civil partnership certificate.

Order several certified copies of the death certificate at the same time, because banks, pension providers and insurers usually want to see an original copy, not a photocopy.

The registrar will also give you the form the funeral director needs before a burial or cremation can go ahead.

Use Tell Us Once

Most councils offer Tell Us Once when you register a death, which lets you report it to many government departments in one go.

It passes the details on to bodies such as HMRC, the Department for Work and Pensions, HM Passport Office, the DVLA and the local council, so benefits, the State Pension, tax and the driving licence are dealt with.

The registrar gives you a unique reference number, and you can then use the service online or by phone.

Tell Us Once does not cover banks, private pensions, insurers, utility companies or employers, so you still need to contact those yourself.

Banks, pensions and insurance

Most banks and building societies have a bereavement team, and the free Death Notification Service lets you notify several of them at once.

Accounts held only in the person's name are usually frozen, while joint accounts normally pass to the surviving holder.

Each bank sets its own limit for how much it will release without a grant of probate, so ask each one rather than assuming.

Contact any workplace or personal pension providers and life insurers directly, as some pay a lump sum or a pension to a partner or nominated person.

Probate and the estate

If there is a will, it should name the executors, who are the people responsible for dealing with the estate.

In England and Wales, executors may need to apply for a grant of probate, or a grant of letters of administration if there is no will, and in Scotland the equivalent is called confirmation.

Inheritance Tax may be due if the estate is worth more than the GBP 325,000 nil rate band, and the GOV.UK estimate tool helps you work out whether you need to report it.

Probate and tax rules have many exceptions, so use the GOV.UK guidance and take professional advice if the estate is complicated or there is a dispute.

The home and the household bills

Tell the council about the death, as an empty property left by someone who has died may be exempt from council tax for a period, and a surviving adult living alone can claim the 25% single person discount.

Give the energy, water, broadband and phone companies a meter reading and the date of death, and ask them to close the account or move it into another name.

Cancel or transfer the TV licence, home and car insurance, subscriptions and any direct debits, and ask Royal Mail about redirecting post if the home will be empty.

If the person rented, tell the landlord or housing association, as the tenancy may pass to a partner or need to be ended.

Make it easier for your own family

Much of the stress comes from not knowing where things are, such as the will, the bank accounts, the pensions and who to call.

A written record of your accounts, policies, contacts and funeral wishes, kept somewhere your family knows about, saves them weeks of searching.
